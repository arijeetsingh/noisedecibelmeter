Decibels (dB) measure sound intensity on a logarithmic scale, which means a jump from 70 dB to 80 dB isn't "a bit louder" — it's roughly twice as loud to the human ear. That's why a quick glance at a number doesn't always tell the full story.

## Everyday sound levels

- **0–20 dB** — Near silence, an anechoic chamber
- **20–40 dB** — Whisper, rustling leaves, a quiet library
- **40–60 dB** — Normal conversation, quiet office, rainfall
- **60–70 dB** — Busy restaurant, television at normal volume
- **70–85 dB** — Vacuum cleaner, heavy traffic, loud music
- **85–100 dB** — Power tools, motorcycles — the hearing-damage risk zone begins
- **100–120 dB** — Concerts, jackhammers, construction sites
- **120–140 dB** — Jet engine at close range, threshold of pain

## When does noise become harmful?

Hearing damage is a function of both **loudness** and **exposure time**. OSHA guidelines permit:

- 8 hours at 85 dB
- 4 hours at 90 dB
- 2 hours at 95 dB
- 1 hour at 100 dB

Every 3 dB increase roughly halves the safe exposure time. That's why a 100 dB concert for two hours carries the same risk budget as an 85 dB office for a full workday.

## Measuring your own environment

Open the [decibel meter](/) in your browser, grant microphone access, and watch the live reading. Use dBA weighting for general environments (it approximates human hearing sensitivity) and dBC for bass-heavy or low-frequency sources like machinery or subwoofers.

If you're consistently seeing readings above 85 dB in a space you spend hours in — an open-plan office near machinery, a workshop, a live-music venue — it's worth taking action: hearing protection, distance from the source, or raising the issue with whoever manages the space.

## The takeaway

A browser-based decibel meter won't replace a calibrated hardware unit for legal compliance work, but it's more than accurate enough to answer the question most people actually have: *is this environment loud enough to worry about?* If the number is consistently above 85 dB, the answer is yes.
